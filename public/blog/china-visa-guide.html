China Visa Guide 2026: Tourist (L) Visa, 240-Hour Transit & Application Steps

Updated July 2026 • 12 min read

Getting a China visa in 2026 is straightforward if you apply with the right documents and enough lead time. Most leisure travelers need the Tourist (L) visa, while many short-stay visitors from eligible countries can now skip the embassy entirely using the expanded 240-hour visa-free transit policy. This guide walks through both routes, the exact paperwork, realistic timelines and fees, and the common mistakes that get applications rejected.

Once your visa is sorted, our China travel guides help you turn approval into a real itinerary across Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an, Hangzhou, and Dali.

Do You Even Need a Visa?

Start here, because the answer saves time and money. You need a tourist (L) visa unless:

  • You hold a passport from a country with a mutual visa-waiver agreement (e.g., Singapore, Brunei, and Japan for short stays — rules shift, so verify with the Chinese embassy in your country).
  • You qualify for the 240-hour visa-free transit (see below) on an onward ticket.
  • You enter Hong Kong or Macau, which have separate, more relaxed entry rules for many nationalities.

If none apply, proceed with the L visa application below.

The Tourist (L) Visa at a Glance

The L visa is issued for tourism. Since late 2023, China has resumed multi-entry L visas for many nationalities, and standard validity is often:

  • Single entry: 30 days, valid 3 months from issue.
  • Double entry: two 30-day visits within 6 months.
  • Multiple entry: 30, 60, or 90 days per visit, valid 6 months to 10 years depending on nationality (U.S. citizens commonly receive a 10-year multi-entry L visa).

Note: the validity (how long the visa is good to enter) is different from the duration of stay (how many days per visit). A 10-year visa with 60-day stays means you can visit many times, each up to 60 days.

Required Documents for a China Tourist Visa

Requirements vary slightly by consulate, but the core set is consistent:

  1. Passport: Valid at least 6 months beyond entry, with two blank visa pages.
  2. Visa application form: Completed online at the official COVA (China Online Visa Application) portal, then printed and signed.
  3. Passport photo: One recent 48×33 mm color photo, white background, glued (not stapled) to the form.
  4. Proof of itinerary: Round-trip flight reservation and hotel bookings covering your stay. You do not need to pay yet — a refundable reservation is fine.
  5. Bank statement: Some consulates ask for 3–6 months of statements showing sufficient funds.
  6. Invitation letter: Usually not required for pure tourism, but helpful if you're staying with friends or visiting non-standard regions.
Tip: Application rules differ by location. Many countries route through a third-party "China Visa Application Service Center" (CVASC) rather than the embassy directly. Check your nearest center's website — they publish the exact local checklist and photo specs.

Step-by-Step China Visa Application

Step 1 — Complete the COVA form online

Go to the COVA portal, select your embassy/consulate jurisdiction, and fill in your details exactly as they appear on your passport. List your planned cities and dates. Print the confirmation page with the barcode.

Step 2 — Book an appointment

Through the CVASC or embassy system, reserve a drop-off slot. In busy seasons (spring, summer, before National Day), slots fill 2–3 weeks out. Walk-in emergency service exists but costs more.

Step 3 — Submit documents and biometrics

Attend in person (or authorize a proxy/agency). Expect fingerprinting and a photo at the center. Keep your pickup receipt — you'll need the barcode to collect the passport.

Step 4 — Pay and collect

Fees are paid on collection (or at submission, depending on the center). Processing typically takes 4–7 business days for standard service; express (2–3 days) and rush (1 day) incur extra charges.

China Visa Fees and Processing Time

Fees depend on nationality because China uses reciprocity. Approximate U.S. tourist visa fees are around US$140 (single/double) and up to US$185 for multiple entry; UK around £85–£170; EU citizens roughly €60–€130. These are embassy fees only — CVASC service fees and courier/agency costs are separate. Always confirm current fees on the official center site, as they change.

The 240-Hour Visa-Free Transit (TWOV)

China's transit-without-visa program expanded in late 2024 and now covers 240 hours (10 days) for eligible passport holders arriving from (and departing to) a third country or region, via designated ports. This is ideal for a Beijing–Shanghai stopover or a longer multi-city loop, as long as you keep moving.

Key rules

  • Eligible nationalities: Roughly 54 countries including the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, most EU states, and others. Verify your country on the official list.
  • Itinerary: You must arrive from one country/region and depart to a different one. For example: Tokyo → Shanghai → Seoul, or London → Beijing → Hong Kong. A round-trip to the same country does not qualify.
  • Onward ticket: A confirmed seat (flight, train, or cruise) leaving mainland China within 240 hours, shown at check-in and immigration.
  • Entry ports: Major airports (Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Chengdu, Xi'an, Hangzhou, and more) plus some land and sea ports. Not every border crossing qualifies — check the published port list.
  • Stay area: You may travel between the approved provinces/cities covered by the policy; you are no longer confined to a single city.
Tip: Tell the airline staff at your departure airport that you plan to use 240-hour TWOV. They must confirm eligibility and add a note, or they may refuse boarding. Arrive early and have your onward ticket printed.

Other Common Visa Types (Briefly)

  • Q visa (family): For visiting Chinese relatives; requires an invitation from the family member.
  • M visa (business): For trade and commercial activities; needs a Chinese company invitation.
  • X visa (study): For enrolled students, with a university admission letter.
  • Z visa (work): For employment, with a work permit notice.

For tourism, stick with L. Don't apply for a business visa just to tour — it complicates future applications.

Common Mistakes That Cause Rejection or Delay

  • Wrong jurisdiction: Applying at a center outside your residence area. Consulates serve specific states/regions.
  • Photo errors: Wrong size, old photo, or stapled instead of glued. This is the #1 reason for rejection.
  • Vague itinerary: Missing hotel bookings or mismatched flight dates raise red flags.
  • Last-minute application: Applying less than two weeks before departure risks missing your trip during peak season.
  • Passport validity: Under 6 months left, or no blank visa pages.

Visa-Free and Easy-Entry Exceptions to Know

Beyond the 240-hour transit rule, a few targeted exemptions can save you a full application:

  • Hainan: Citizens of many countries can visit Hainan island visa-free for up to 30 days on a package or group arrangement — a great warm-weather add-on.
  • Cruise passengers: Foreign tourist groups arriving by cruise at specified ports (e.g., Shanghai, Tianjin) may get 15-day visa-free entry.
  • Regional visa-waiver agreements: Holders of passports from a growing list of countries (Singapore, Brunei, and others on rotation) may enter visa-free for short stays. These change frequently — always confirm with the Chinese embassy in your country before booking.

None of these replace a standard tourist visa for a mainland multi-city trip, but they matter if your plans are regional or cruise-based.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Can I extend a tourist visa inside China? Sometimes, via the local Entry-Exit Bureau, but approval is not guaranteed. It's far safer to apply for the right duration upfront.
  • Do I need a visa if I only transit through a city? If you stay airside and don't pass immigration, no. If you want to leave the airport, use 240-hour TWOV.
  • Is a printed application enough? No — you must submit the COVA form online first, then print the barcode confirmation page to bring in person.
  • Can an agency apply for me? Yes, a proxy or licensed agency can submit on your behalf in most jurisdictions, useful if you're far from a center.

Timeline: When to Start

Begin the China visa application at least 4–6 weeks before departure. That leaves room for document corrections, appointment waits, and standard processing. If you're using 240-hour transit, you can plan much closer to departure — just confirm your onward ticket and eligible ports.
