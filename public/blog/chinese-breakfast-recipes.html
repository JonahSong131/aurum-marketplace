10 Classic Chinese Breakfast Recipes to Make at Home

Updated July 2026 • 13 min read

A real Chinese breakfast is nothing like the fried rice you get at dinner. It's warm, soft, savory, and built around congee, steamed buns, soy milk, and fresh noodles — eaten at street stalls from Beijing to Guangzhou. The good news: almost all of it is easy to recreate in a Western kitchen with a few pantry staples. Here are 10 classic Chinese breakfast recipes you can make at home, with the practical steps that actually matter.

1. Congee (Bái Zhōu / Plain Rice Porridge)

The foundation of Chinese morning food. Unlike Western oatmeal, congee is rice slow-cooked until it breaks down into a silky gruel.

  • Ratio: 1 part short-grain rice to 8–10 parts water.
  • Rinse the rice, add to cold water with a pinch of salt, bring to a boil, then simmer 45–60 min, stirring occasionally. For creamier texture, freeze the rinsed rice for 1 hour first — the cracked grains release starch faster.
  • Toppings: century egg and pork (pí dàn shòu ròu zhōu), sliced ginger, scallions, youtiao pieces, or preserved mustard greens.
Congee tip: A pressure cooker or rice cooker "congee" setting makes this hands-free. Use the "porridge" mode and walk away.

2. Steamed Pork Buns (Bāozi)

Pillowy buns with a juicy pork-and-ginger filling. The dough is the skill; the filling is simple.

  • Dough: 500 g all-purpose flour, 5 g instant yeast, 1 tsp sugar, ~250 ml warm water. Knead 10 min, rest until doubled (1 hr).
  • Filling: 300 g ground pork, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tsp sesame oil, 1 tbsp grated ginger, 2 scallions, a splash of cooled gelatin-rich broth (for "soup" buns). Mix one direction until sticky.
  • Roll dough into balls, flatten, fill, pleat, and steam 12–15 min over boiling water. Don't open the lid for 5 min after.

3. Youtiao (Fried Dough Sticks)

The quintessential partner to soy milk — long golden strips with a hollow, chewy-crisp bite.

  • Dough: 250 g flour, 1 tsp baking powder, 1/2 tsp baking soda, 1 egg, 120 ml water, 1 tbsp oil. Mix, rest 2 hr (or overnight in the fridge).
  • Roll thin, cut into strips, press two together, and stretch to 25 cm.
  • Fry at 190°C, turning, until deep golden. Serve fresh — they lose crunch fast.

4. Soy Milk (Dòujiāng)

Homemade soy milk beats the carton. Serve sweet (sugar) or savory (salt, scallion, a dash of soy — the northern style).

  • Soak 200 g dried soybeans overnight. Blend with 1 L water, strain through a nut-milk bag.
  • Boil the liquid 10–15 min (raw soy milk is toxic — must boil), skimming foam. Sweeten or salt to taste.
  • Dip youtiao in it for the classic combo.

5. Rice Noodle Rolls (Chǎngfěn / Cheung Fun)

Cantonese steamed rice sheets, silky and savory, rolled around shrimp, beef, or scallion.

  • Batter: 1:2 ratio rice flour to water, plus a splash of wheat starch for elasticity.
  • Pour a thin layer into a greased plate, steam 2–3 min until set, loosen with a spatula, roll up.
  • Sauce: light soy, sesame oil, sugar, and a little water, drizzled on top with toasted sesame.

6. Jianbing (Savory Breakfast Crepe)

Beijing's famous street crepe — crackly, eggy, stuffed with crispy crackers and hoisin.

  • Batter: mung bean flour + water (plus a little wheat flour), thinner than pancake batter.
  • Pour onto a hot griddle, crack an egg, spread, flip, brush with hoisin and chili, add báocuì (crispy fried cracker) and scallions, fold, and roll.
  • Hard to perfect without a flat griddle, but a nonstick pan works for a home version.

7. Mantou (Steamed Plain Buns)

The unsweetened cousin of baozi — soft, slightly sweet, perfect for dipping in congee or savory sauces.

  • 500 g flour, 5 g yeast, 1 tsp sugar, 230 ml water. Knead, rise 1 hr, shape into oval rolls, steam 15 min.
  • Also great pan-fried into jiān mántou (crisp bottom, soft top) with condensed milk for dipping.

8. Tea Eggs (Cháyè Dàn)

Hard-boiled eggs simmered in black tea and spices — mahogany-marbled, gently savory, make-ahead friendly.

  • Boil 6 eggs 7 min, crack the shells all over (don't peel).
  • Simmer in water with 3 black tea bags, 2 star anise, 1 cinnamon stick, 1 tbsp soy sauce, 1 tsp salt, for 1 hr. Steep overnight for deeper flavor.

9. Wonton Soup (Húntun)

Silky little dumplings in clear broth — a warming breakfast in the south.

  • Filling: ground pork + shrimp, ginger, scallion, soy, sesame oil.
  • Wrap a teaspoon in each wonton skin; boil 3–4 min until they float.
  • Broth: chicken stock, white pepper, a drop of sesame oil, topped with scallions.

10. Scallion Pancakes (Cōngyóubǐng)

Flaky, layered, pan-fried — crisp outside, chewy inside, loaded with scallion.

  • Dough: 300 g flour, 180 ml hot water, rest 30 min. Roll thin, brush with oil, scatter scallions and salt, roll up like a rope, coil, flatten.
  • Pan-fry in oil over medium heat until both sides are blistered and golden, ~3 min per side.

Build a Chinese Breakfast Spread at Home

You don't need all ten at once. A typical home breakfast is:

  • The warm base: a bowl of congee or a cup of soy milk.
  • The carb: a steamed bun (baozi or mantou) or scallion pancake.
  • The crunch: youtiao or a tea egg.
  • The fresh note: pickled vegetables or scallions.

Most of these keep or freeze well. Make a batch of baozi and youtiao dough on Sunday, and weekday breakfasts become a 10-minute steam.

Pantry starters: Keep soy sauce, sesame oil, rice flour, mung bean flour, star anise, and dried soybeans on hand. With those, eight of the ten recipes above are within reach any morning.

Drinks and Sides to Complete the Meal

A Chinese breakfast is rarely just the star dish. Round it out with:

  • Warm soy milk (sweet or savory) — the default drink, sipped alongside youtiao.
  • Congee side pickles: Zha cai (preserved mustard tuber) or sulafu (pickled cabbage) add salty crunch. Find them in jars at any Asian market.
  • Plain water or chrysanthemum tea: Light, palate-cleansing, and traditional.
  • A piece of fruit: Mandarin, pomelo, or sliced apple to finish.

Where to Buy Ingredients in a Western Supermarket

You don't need a specialty store for most of these. Here's the shortcut:

  • Asian market (best): Doubanjiang, rice flour, mung bean flour, dried soybeans, star anise, and frozen baozi dough. One trip covers everything.
  • Regular supermarket: All-purpose flour, yeast, eggs, scallions, ginger, soy sauce, and sesame oil are all mainstream now. Youtiao can be faked with frozen pastry dough.
  • Online: Bamboo steamer baskets, a nut-milk bag for soy milk, and rice-flour blends ship easily and last months.

Most recipes above use the same five staples — flour, yeast, soy sauce, sesame oil, and scallions — so a single shop sets up your whole week.

Most of these dishes also freeze beautifully. Par-cook baozi and mantou, freeze on a tray, then steam straight from frozen for 18 minutes — your weekday breakfast becomes a 20-minute ritual instead of a project. Congee reheats with a splash of water; youtiao is the only item best eaten within the hour it's fried. Regional note: the north leans savory (fermented tofu, pickles, soy milk), while the south prefers sweet congee, rice rolls, and steamed custard — mix both traditions to keep breakfast interesting.
